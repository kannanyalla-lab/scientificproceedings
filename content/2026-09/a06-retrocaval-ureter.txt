## Introduction
Retrocaval ureter, also termed circumcaval ureter, is a rare congenital anomaly resulting from abnormal embryological development of the inferior vena cava (IVC). The ureter passes posterior to the IVC and may become compressed, resulting in varying degrees of proximal hydroureteronephrosis. The anomaly predominantly affects the right ureter because of the embryological development of the IVC. Although many patients are asymptomatic, obstruction may present with flank pain, recurrent urinary tract infection, or deterioration of renal drainage.^1,2^

Historically, open excision of the retrocaval segment followed by anterior repositioning and ureteroureterostomy was the standard treatment. Laparoscopic and robotic techniques have subsequently emerged as minimally invasive alternatives. Comparative data suggest that robotic reconstruction can reduce operative time while maintaining comparable perioperative outcomes.^3,4^

## Case presentation
An 18-year-old female was evaluated for irregular menstrual cycles and was incidentally found to have right-sided hydronephrosis. She had no flank pain, dysuria, frequency, hematuria, or fever. There was no history of previous abdominal, pelvic, or urological surgery. On examination, the abdomen was soft and non-tender, with no palpable mass. Routine laboratory investigations were normal. Urine culture grew *Escherichia coli*, for which she received appropriate antibiotic treatment.

CT urography performed at an outside hospital in August 2025 demonstrated good contrast excretion from both kidneys. The right renal pelvicalyceal system and upper third of the ureter were dilated. The dilated right upper ureter was traced to approximately the L4 vertebral level, where it deviated medially and passed posterior to the IVC, a characteristic configuration confirming the diagnosis of a right retrocaval ureter. The left ureter and urinary bladder were normal. The imaging findings were particularly important for operative planning because they demonstrated the exact anatomical relationship between the ureter and the IVC before reconstruction.^5^

Despite the absence of symptoms, the presence of hydronephrosis and the abnormal obstructive course prompted consideration of reconstructive surgery. The patient underwent robotic repair after counselling regarding the nature of the anomaly, available treatment options, and the risks of reconstructive ureteric surgery.

## Robotic surgical technique and postoperative course
The patient was placed in the left lateral position using a four-port robotic configuration. Following medial reflection of the colon and kocherization of the duodenum, the IVC and right ureter were clearly visualized. The proximal and distal ureter were dissected carefully with preservation of periureteral blood supply, and the retrocaval segment was mobilized. The key technical objective was to release the ureter from its retrocaval course while preserving its vascularity. After adequate mobilization, the abnormal retrocaval segment was excised, and the ureter was repositioned anterior to the IVC without tension. The ends were spatulated, and an intracorporeal end-to-end ureteroureterostomy was completed over a 6 Fr double-J stent using 3-0 Vicryl sutures. The use of robotic wristed instruments facilitated needle handling and precise suturing around the major vascular structure.^3,4,6^

The postoperative recovery was uncomplicated. There were no intraoperative complications and no drain was required. The Foley catheter was removed on postoperative day 2, and the patient was discharged on postoperative day 3. The double-J stent was removed at 6 weeks postoperatively. At the time of this report, the patient remains asymptomatic on regular clinical follow-up.

## Discussion
Retrocaval ureter is an uncommon congenital anomaly resulting from abnormal venous development, in which the ureter passes posterior to the IVC. Modern cross-sectional imaging has substantially improved preoperative diagnosis and anatomical definition. CT urography is particularly useful for demonstrating the characteristic ureteric course and associated hydronephrosis.^1,5^

Management depends on severity of symptoms, degree of obstruction, renal function, and the extent or progression of hydronephrosis. In patients requiring surgical reconstruction, the fundamental principles are excision of the compressed retrocaval segment, anterior repositioning of the ureter, and a tension-free ureteric anastomosis. Laparoscopic series have demonstrated favorable functional outcomes, while robotic surgery may facilitate intracorporeal suturing and dissection in this anatomically demanding location.^4,7^

The present case is notable because the patient was young and clinically asymptomatic, with the anomaly detected incidentally. The decision to proceed with reconstruction was based on the presence of hydronephrosis and the obstructive anatomical configuration. Previous reports have similarly described asymptomatic patients in whom retrocaval ureter was diagnosed incidentally on imaging.^6,8^

In a comparative study, Temiz et al. reported a mean operative time of 92 minutes for robotic repair compared with 190 minutes for laparoscopic repair, with broadly comparable blood loss, drain removal, and hospital stay.^3^ The operative time of 96 minutes in the present case is consistent with the reported efficiency of robotic reconstruction. A recent robot-assisted case report in a young woman also described successful reconstruction without perioperative complications and resolution of hydronephrosis on follow-up.^6^

The rarity of retrocaval ureter means that evidence remains predominantly based on case reports and small series. Therefore, the choice between laparoscopic and robotic approaches should be individualized based on surgeon experience, available technology, patient factors, and cost considerations.^4,7^

## Conclusion
Robotic excision of the retrocaval ureteric segment with anterior repositioning and ureteroureterostomy is a safe and technically feasible minimally invasive procedure. In this young asymptomatic female with incidentally detected hydronephrosis, the procedure was completed in 96 minutes with minimal blood loss, no complications, and an uncomplicated early postoperative recovery. The case highlights the potential utility of robotic visualization in facilitating precise ureteric dissection, anterior repositioning, and intracorporeal ureteroureterostomy.
