--- abstract
Obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) is common and frequently undertreated, not because effective therapies are lacking, but because matching treatment to the individual patient requires more than a reflexive prescription for continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP). This review presents a concise clinical framework for OSA management: the pathophysiologic principles underlying treatment selection, CPAP as first-line therapy, and the expanding range of alternatives - oral appliances, positional therapy, surgery, hypoglossal nerve stimulation, and pharmacotherapy - that enable treatment to be tailored rather than applied uniformly. This review gives particular attention to two pharmacologic developments: tirzepatide, approved for OSA in adults with obesity, and AD109, an investigational agent that acts through a distinct neuromuscular mechanism and has shown promising phase 3 results.

## Introduction
The clinical challenge in OSA is rarely the diagnosis itself; it is sustaining patients on effective therapy beyond the first few months. The scale of the problem remains considerable: an estimated 425 million adults aged 30-69 years have moderate-to-severe OSA worldwide, with nearly one billion affected once mild disease is included.^1^ OSA is linked to hypertension, atrial fibrillation, heart failure, stroke, type 2 diabetes, and increased mortality.^1^ Despite this, many affected individuals remain undiagnosed, and many of those diagnosed discontinue first-line therapy within a year. This review provides a practical framework covering the pathophysiology and diagnosis of OSA, with a primary focus on the management options available once OSA is confirmed.

## Pathophysiology
The pharynx collapses during sleep when forces promoting airway closure - negative inspiratory pressure, soft-tissue weight, and peri-airway fat in obesity - exceed the dilating action of the genioglossus and other upper airway muscles. Although anatomical factors such as retrognathia, macroglossia, tonsillar hypertrophy, and a narrow oropharynx predispose to collapse, they do not fully explain OSA heterogeneity. Eckert and colleagues identified four non-anatomical "endotypic" traits that contribute to disease susceptibility and severity: a low arousal threshold, high loop gain (unstable ventilatory control), reduced pharyngeal dilator muscle responsiveness, and low waking lung volume.^2^

These traits have clinical relevance because their relative contribution varies between patients and may influence treatment response. A lean patient with high loop gain is unlikely to respond to an oral appliance the way an anatomically obstructed patient would, which explains why newer pharmacologic agents (see Pharmacotherapy) target the neuromuscular component directly. Repeated apneas and hypopneas produce cycles of hypoxemia-reoxygenation and arousal, driving sympathetic activation, inflammation, and endothelial dysfunction - the substrate linking OSA to cardiometabolic disease.^1,2^ Figure 1 summarizes this sequence.

## Diagnosis
Classic symptoms - snoring, witnessed apneas, daytime sleepiness - remain common, but many patients, particularly women and older adults, present atypically with fatigue, morning headache, or mood change. STOP-BANG and the Berlin Questionnaire can help identify patients at increased risk before objective testing.^3^ Diagnosis requires objective sleep testing: in-laboratory polysomnography remains the reference standard, particularly for patients with significant comorbidities or an inconclusive home sleep apnea test, whereas home sleep apnea testing is appropriate for adults with a high pretest probability of moderate to severe OSA and no major comorbidity.^3^ Severity is graded by the apnea-hypopnea index (AHI): mild 5-14.9 events/hour, moderate 15-29.9 events/hour, severe ≥30 events/hour. AHI should be interpreted alongside symptom burden, comorbidities, and other clinical factors rather than in isolation.^3^

## Principles of treatment selection
Management should be individualized: address modifiable risk factors, including weight, sleep position, alcohol, sedatives, and smoking; select a primary airway therapy based on disease severity, anatomy, and likely adherence; treat accompanying comorbidities; and establish structured follow-up to assess both disease control and continued treatment use.

## Positive airway pressure therapy
CPAP remains first-line therapy for most patients with moderate-to-severe OSA, pneumatically splinting the airway open.^4^ Evidence consistently demonstrates improvements in sleepiness, quality of life, and blood pressure, whereas evidence for reduction in major cardiovascular outcomes is less consistent.^4^ The inconsistent evidence for cardiovascular outcomes may in part reflect limited adherence in clinical trials. Auto-titrating PAP, which can be initiated at home, is now a practical default; bilevel PAP is generally reserved for patients intolerant of high fixed pressure or who have coexisting hypoventilation.

Adherence, rather than the device itself, most often determines treatment success. Common barriers include mask discomfort, nasal congestion, claustrophobia, and partner-related concerns. The formal adherence threshold (≥4 hours/night on ≥70% of nights) is a pragmatic regulatory benchmark; incremental use continues to confer benefit up to full-night coverage.^4^ Early follow-up within one to two weeks, heated humidification, alternative mask interfaces, structured education, and device download data can all help improve adherence in practice.

## Oral appliance therapy
Mandibular advancement devices reposition the mandible and tongue base anteriorly, enlarging the retroglossal airway. They are recommended as a first-line option for selected patients with mild-to-moderate OSA who prefer an oral appliance to PAP, and as an alternative for patients who are intolerant of or unable to adhere to PAP.^5^ CPAP produces a greater reduction in AHI on a population basis, but higher adherence with oral appliances can narrow the difference in effectiveness in routine practice. Devices should be custom-fitted by a dentist trained in dental sleep medicine, with follow-up studies to confirm AHI control, rather than relying on symptoms alone.^5^

## Positional therapy and weight management
Patients with strongly position-dependent AHI can benefit from vibrotactile positional trainers that discourage supine sleep, although adherence data remain more limited than for PAP. Weight loss of even 5-10% can measurably reduce AHI, while greater weight loss through lifestyle change, pharmacotherapy, or bariatric surgery can lead to remission in some patients.^6^ However, OSA may persist despite weight loss, particularly when longstanding obesity has contributed to persistent upper airway anatomical or physiological abnormalities.

## Pharmacotherapy
### Tirzepatide
Tirzepatide, a dual GIP/GLP-1 receptor agonist, was approved by the FDA in December 2024 for treatment of moderate-to-severe OSA in adults with obesity, in conjunction with diet and increased physical activity.^7^ In the SURMOUNT-OSA program, tirzepatide produced a substantial reduction in AHI at 52 weeks compared with placebo in both patients unable or unwilling to use PAP and those using PAP.^7^ This is considerably larger than expected from weight loss alone. Tirzepatide therefore represents an important addition (with trial-level evidence) to the treatment landscape for patients with OSA and obesity, particularly those who are unable or unwilling to use PAP.^7^ Caveats remain - and include gradual dose escalation to improve gastrointestinal tolerability, cost and access barriers, and the lack of long-term data on the trajectory of OSA after treatment discontinuation. ### AD109 (Aroxybutynin/Atomoxetine)
AD109 is not yet an approved therapy; it is discussed here because the phase 3 data are now published and clinically relevant. It is a once-daily combination of an antimuscarinic (aroxybutynin) and a norepinephrine reuptake inhibitor (atomoxetine), designed to preserve genioglossus and dilator muscle activity during sleep independent of body weight (Figure 3). In the SynAIRgy and LunAIRo phase 3 trials, which enrolled adults with mild-to-severe OSA who were unable or unwilling to use PAP, AD109 produced a placebo-adjusted AHI reduction of approximately 4 events/hour over 26 weeks, with improvements in oxygen desaturation index and fatigue.^8^ This is more modest than typically seen with CPAP or tirzepatide. Still, a significantly greater proportion of participants receiving AD109 achieved a ≥50% reduction in AHI compared with placebo, with no excess of serious adverse events, and the treatment effect was observed across patients irrespective of body weight.^8^ These findings suggest a potential role for AD109 in patients who cannot tolerate or decline PAP, with few effective alternatives.^9,10^ At the time of writing, AD109 has not been approved by the FDA, the European Medicines Agency, or other regulatory authorities, and this review should not be read as recommending its use outside a clinical trial setting.

## Surgical management
Surgery is generally considered for patients with anatomically correctable obstruction or for those unable to use PAP or an oral appliance. Options include uvulopalatopharyngoplasty, tonsillectomy/adenoidectomy (effective where tonsillar hypertrophy is a major contributor, and first-line surgery in children), maxillomandibular advancement (which can provide among the most durable results, particularly in younger patients), and nasal surgery, which rarely resolves OSA on its own but may improve CPAP tolerance. Drug-induced sleep endoscopy (DISE) has become routine for surgical planning, visualizing the site and pattern of collapse under sedation. In patients with severe obesity, bariatric surgery can produce substantial weight loss and meaningful, though often incomplete, AHI improvement. PAP should generally be continued during the interim.^6^

## Hypoglossal nerve stimulation
Hypoglossal nerve stimulation (HGNS) is an established option for selected patients with moderate-to-severe OSA who cannot tolerate CPAP.^11^ An implanted system senses the respiratory cycle and stimulates the hypoglossal nerve, producing anterior tongue displacement during inspiration. Candidacy requires a DISE excluding complete concentric palatal collapse, BMI below a defined threshold, and AHI in the moderate-to-severe range without a substantial central component; adherence generally exceeds that seen with PAP, given the absence of a nightly mask interface.^11^ HGNS is not currently available, for example, in Bahrain or elsewhere in the region; suitable candidates may therefore require referral abroad where feasible. It is presented here as a potential option for carefully selected, treatment-exhausted patients rather than a locally accessible pathway.

## Special populations
OSA treatment forms part of the broader management strategy in patients with resistant hypertension and in selected patients with recurrent atrial fibrillation after ablation. However, evidence that PAP reduces major cardiovascular outcomes remains inconsistent.^4^ Expectations should therefore be discussed in terms of established benefits, rather than presumed cardiovascular protection. Undiagnosed OSA carries real perioperative risk, warranting proactive screening before major surgery. When obesity hypoventilation coexists with OSA, bilevel PAP should replace CPAP alone, alongside aggressive weight management.^4^

## Long-term follow-up
OSA requires the same longitudinal attention as other chronic diseases, with reassessment of symptoms, treatment adherence, weight, and blood pressure at follow-up visits. Device downloads and telemonitoring can help identify and correct mask leaks or declining use early. Substantial changes in weight or anatomy should prompt reassessment of the original prescription.

## Conclusion
OSA management has moved well beyond a single default prescription of CPAP. CPAP remains the first-line and best-studied therapy for moderate-to-severe disease. Still, oral appliances, positional therapy, surgery, hypoglossal nerve stimulation, and pharmacotherapy each have a role depending on the patient's phenotype, anatomy, and preferences. Tirzepatide has expanded the therapeutic options for adults with obesity and OSA who are unable or unwilling to use PAP.^7^ At the same time, AD109, if its phase 3 findings translate into regulatory approval and broader clinical evidence, could provide a mechanism-based option for patients who cannot use PAP, including those without obesity.^8-10^ None of these developments replace careful phenotyping and an honest discussion with patients about what they are realistically prepared and able to use consistently every night.
