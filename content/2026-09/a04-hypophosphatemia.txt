--- abstract
Intravenous iron sucrose is often perceived as a safer alternative to ferric carboxymaltose but can induce severe symptomatic hypophosphatemia. We report the case of a 44-year-old woman with iron deficiency anemia whose fatigue and dyspnea worsened despite a successful hemoglobin response following iron sucrose infusion. This led to testing her phosphate levels, and she was found to have profound hypophosphatemia (1.4 mg/dL) alongside an inappropriately non-suppressed fibroblast growth factor 23 (FGF23) level. She was successfully treated with phosphate repletion and cholecalciferol. This case challenges the perception of iron sucrose as uniquely safe and underscores the need for clinicians to monitor phosphate levels, particularly after multi-dose regimens, and to consider hypophosphatemia in patients with persistent or worsening symptoms following otherwise successful intravenous (IV) iron therapy.

## Introduction
Iron deficiency anemia (IDA) is a major global health concern, affecting an estimated one-third of the world's population and disproportionately affecting women of reproductive age and individuals with chronic diseases. For patients who cannot tolerate or do not respond to oral iron, or for those requiring rapid repletion, intravenous iron therapy is a standard and highly effective treatment.^1^

Hypophosphatemia is a recognized adverse effect of IV iron therapy. Initially and most prominently reported with ferric carboxymaltose (FCM), this complication was often regarded as a distinctive drawback of this formulation.^2^ This perception was reinforced by randomized controlled trials that demonstrated a significantly higher incidence of hypophosphatemia with FCM (74-78%) compared to iron isomaltoside (0-8%).^3^ Consequently, iron sucrose has often been considered a safer alternative with respect to this complication. Emerging evidence now challenges this notion, confirming that the propensity to induce hypophosphatemia is an inherent characteristic of IV iron therapy as a class. However, its incidence and severity vary among preparations. The pathophysiology is rooted in the administration of IV iron itself, which stimulates the production of the phosphaturic hormone fibroblast growth factor 23 (FGF23).^4^

## Case presentation
A 44-year-old premenopausal woman presented to the emergency department with profound fatigue and occasional dyspnoea on mild exertion. Her medical history included type 2 diabetes mellitus, a previous Transient Ischemic Attack (TIA), significant left Internal Carotid Artery (ICA) stenosis, and dyslipidemia. Following her TIA, she was placed on dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT) with Clopidogrel and Aspirin. She had a history of severe menorrhagia with passage of clots for 3 years, which had developed after initiation of DAPT. She was referred to the hematology outpatient department for evaluation of anemia.

A comprehensive hematological evaluation was performed. A conventional coagulation panel was done because of menorrhagia, and was normal. Red blood cell indices showed a microcytic pattern, confirmed by peripheral blood film revealing hypochromic, microcytic red blood cells, consistent with iron deficiency. The reticulocyte count was low at 1.18%, indicating an inadequate bone marrow response. Iron studies showed depleted ferritin (12.5 μg/L), low serum iron (30 μg/dL), and elevated total iron-binding capacity (TIBC) (434.5 μg/dL), resulting in a profoundly low transferrin saturation of 6.8%. This biochemical profile confirmed severe iron deficiency anemia. She was started on a course of IV iron sucrose, receiving four infusions of 200 mg each on alternate days. Following completion of therapy, her haemoglobin increased to 12.8 g/dL, indicating a successful haematological response.

Despite correction of her anemia, she subsequently returned to the clinic with worsening fatigue and shortness of breath on exertion. Her vital signs were stable with normal oxygen tension in ambient air, and her ECG and troponin levels were normal. The laboratory workup during this admission revealed severe hypophosphatemia, with a serum phosphorus level of 1.4 mg/dL. However, the random urine phosphorus level was lower (16.7 mg/dL). IV iron sucrose-associated hypophosphatemia was considered the most likely diagnosis.

Serum fibroblast growth factor 23 (FGF23) concentration was within the high-normal range at 75.71 RU/mL (reference range: 23.20-95.40 RU/mL), despite profound hypophosphatemia, which should normally suppress FGF23 production entirely. Further metabolic workup revealed a decreased vitamin D level of 28.3 ng/mL, indicating insufficiency, while serum calcium and magnesium levels were within normal limits.

Although hypophosphatemia is reported more frequently and prominently with ferric carboxymaltose (FCM), severe hypophosphatemia can rarely occur following iron sucrose administration. Concomitant vitamin D insufficiency, along with elevated FGF23, may have contributed to the development or persistence of hypophosphatemia in this patient. This complication is important to recognize in the era of parenteral iron therapy.

In view of her symptoms and the severity of hypophosphatemia, acute phosphate replacement was initiated. For acute IV repletion, she received an intravenous infusion of potassium phosphate. She was transitioned to oral phosphate supplementation with potassium phosphate sachets three times daily. A repeat serum phosphorus level improved to 2.2 mg/dL following initial treatment. Given her concomitant vitamin D insufficiency, she was also prescribed weekly cholecalciferol to address potential contributory factors to bone metabolism and facilitate phosphate absorption. The patient reported significant improvement in her symptoms, with resolution of dyspnea and tiredness within 48 hours of initiating therapy. She was discharged with close outpatient follow-up.

## Discussion
The pathophysiology of hypophosphatemia following intravenous (IV) iron therapy centers on FGF23, a hormone produced predominantly by osteocytes that regulates phosphate and vitamin D metabolism.^4^ Its physiological actions include promoting phosphaturia by downregulating sodium-phosphate cotransporters in the renal proximal tubule and suppressing the conversion of vitamin D to its active form, calcitriol. Intravenous iron administration triggers a rapid and sustained increase in levels of intact biologically active FGF23, likely stimulated by the sudden surge in bioavailable iron. In this case, the pretreatment iron studies confirmed severe deficiency, and the post-treatment hematological response confirmed effective repletion. An inappropriately high normal FGF23 level during this episode of severe hypophosphatemia may be proof of this mechanism.

The development of severe symptomatic hypophosphatemia after a multi-dose course of iron sucrose is consistent with emerging evidence that this complication can occur with other IV iron formulations. The onset of symptoms following the fourth infusion suggests the documented cumulative dose-effect relationship on FGF23 production. The patient's symptoms of fatigue and dyspnea, which persisted after a successful hematological response (Hb 10.2 g/dL), are a classic red flag for iatrogenic hypophosphatemia. This is a critical diagnostic pitfall, as these symptoms can mimic anemia and be misattributed to treatment failure.

This case has several implications for clinical practice. Iron sucrose should not be assumed to be free of clinically significant effects on phosphate metabolism, as all IV iron formulations carry this risk. Clinicians should actively monitor serum phosphate levels before starting IV iron therapy and again 1-2 weeks after completing a course, particularly in patients receiving multiple doses or with preexisting risk factors. There is need for more awareness to be raised among both patients and clinicians about the nonspecific symptoms of hypophosphatemia. This can lead to early diagnosis and intervention. Management involves phosphate repletion, with IV therapy reserved for severe or symptomatic cases. However, because the primary driver is renal wasting, supplementation can be challenging and requires close monitoring to avoid both undertreatment and the risks associated with overtreatment, such as hypocalcemia and metastatic calcification.^5^

## Conclusion
The key take-home message is the need for heightened vigilance. As the use of intravenous iron therapy continues to increase, hypophosphatemia should be considered a potential complication, even with formulations such as iron sucrose that are generally perceived to carry a lower risk. Close monitoring, a high index of clinical suspicion, and timely recognition and management are essential to prevent morbidity and optimize the safety and effectiveness of IV iron therapy.
