--- abstract Introduction
Coagulation factor deficiencies are rare disorders that may first become apparent during pregnancy and are associated with an increased risk of hemorrhage in the antepartum, intrapartum, and postpartum periods. Although pregnancy induces a physiological hypercoagulable state, women with inherited or acquired coagulation factor deficiencies remain susceptible to bleeding complications. Early diagnosis and multidisciplinary management are essential to optimize maternal and neonatal outcomes.
--- abstract Case reports
We report two cases of previously undiagnosed coagulation factor deficiencies identified during pregnancy. The first case involved a 24-year-old woman with recurrent antepartum bleeding and progressive subchorionic hemorrhage beginning in early pregnancy. Routine coagulation studies were normal, but factor assay revealed severe factor XIII deficiency. She was successfully managed with weekly cryoprecipitate transfusions and tranexamic acid, resulting in a term delivery without excessive bleeding. The second case involved a 35-year-old woman who was found to have an isolated prolongation of activated partial thromboplastin time during routine antenatal evaluation. Further investigations demonstrated severe factor XI deficiency, which was subsequently confirmed by genetic testing. Following prophylactic fresh frozen plasma transfusion, she underwent an uncomplicated vaginal delivery and had an uneventful postpartum course.
--- abstract Discussion
These cases highlight the diverse clinical presentations of coagulation factor deficiencies in pregnancy and the limitations of routine coagulation screening. Accurate diagnosis requires a high index of suspicion and the use of targeted coagulation factor assays. Management should be individualized and involve close collaboration among maternal fetal medicine specialists, obstetricians, hematologists, anesthesiologists, and neonatologists.
--- abstract Conclusion
Timely recognition and multidisciplinary management of coagulation factor deficiencies can result in favorable maternal and neonatal outcomes, even in women whose disorders first become apparent during pregnancy.

## Introduction
Bleeding disorders include coagulation factor deficiencies (both congenital and acquired), platelet disorders, fibrinolytic defects, and vascular disorders. These conditions may manifest as spontaneous or provoked bleeding or may remain asymptomatic. Normal pregnancy is characterized by a procoagulant state. Important changes include increased levels of factors VII, VIII, X, XII, and fibrinogen; decreased levels of factors XI and XIII; increased von Willebrand factor (vWF); reduced protein S activity; and reduced fibrinolytic activity due to increased plasminogen activator inhibitors. These physiological adaptations prepare the body for delivery and placental separation. In women with coagulation factor deficiencies, however, this hemostatic balance is disturbed, and the risk of hemorrhage may persist throughout pregnancy, delivery, and the postpartum period.^1^

Von Willebrand disease (vWD) is the most common inherited disorder of hemostasis and results from quantitative (types 1 and 3) or qualitative (type 2) defects of von Willebrand factor (VWF). Depending on the vWD subtype, inheritance may be autosomal dominant or recessive. Physiological increases in VWF and factor VIII levels during pregnancy may normalize these levels, particularly in women with type 1 and some forms of type 2 disease. Following delivery, these levels decline rapidly, increasing the risk of postpartum hemorrhage and the need for blood product transfusion. Management during childbirth and the postpartum period includes desmopressin or replacement therapy with VWF-containing factor VIII concentrates.^2^

Hemophilia is inherited in an X-linked recessive manner and primarily affects males, while females are usually carriers, although some may be affected. During pregnancy, factor VIII levels may increase in hemophilia A carriers, whereas factor IX levels in hemophilia B carriers generally remain unchanged. Consequently, these women remain at risk of antepartum, intrapartum, and postpartum hemorrhage. Assessment of factor levels during the third trimester, careful delivery planning, avoidance of traumatic obstetric procedures whenever possible, and appropriate factor replacement therapy are essential to optimize maternal and neonatal outcomes.

Factor XI deficiency is particularly prevalent among individuals of Ashkenazi Jewish ancestry, although it occurs in all populations. Unlike hemophilia, the severity of bleeding correlates poorly with plasma factor levels, and clinical manifestations vary considerably among affected individuals. Factor XI levels generally remain stable during pregnancy and do not exhibit the physiological increase observed with some other coagulation factors. Consequently, affected women remain at risk of bleeding complications throughout pregnancy and the puerperium. Postpartum hemorrhage is the most important obstetric complication, and excessive bleeding may occur following cesarean section, operative vaginal delivery, or other invasive procedures. Management should be individualized according to the patient's bleeding history, factor XI level, and planned mode of delivery. Women with a significant bleeding history or those undergoing high-risk procedures may require replacement therapy with factor XI concentrate, where available, or fresh frozen plasma (FFP). Antifibrinolytic agents such as tranexamic acid are useful adjuncts and are commonly administered during the postpartum period to reduce the risk of secondary postpartum hemorrhage.^3^

Deficiencies of factors I, II, V, VII, X, XIII, and other rare coagulation factors may result in significant obstetric complications, including recurrent miscarriage, placental abruption, and severe postpartum hemorrhage. Factor XIII deficiency is particularly associated with recurrent pregnancy loss because of its essential role in placental implantation and maintenance.^4^ Management depends on the specific deficiency and generally involves replacement therapy with factor concentrates, fresh frozen plasma, or cryoprecipitate.

Because coagulation factor deficiency disorders are uncommon, many clinicians encounter them infrequently. Awareness of their clinical manifestations and management is essential for ensuring safe obstetric care. Here, we report two cases of rare coagulation factor deficiencies with distinct clinical presentations that were recognized for the first time during pregnancy.

## Case reports
### Case 1
A 24-year-old second gravida, with no personal or family history of bleeding diathesis and a previous uneventful pregnancy resulting in a term cesarean section, presented with multiple episodes of vaginal bleeding since 10 weeks of gestation. The bleeding was unresponsive to progesterone therapy, and serial ultrasonographic examinations demonstrated progressively increasing subchorionic hemorrhage. Investigations revealed moderate anemia with a normal platelet count, normal conventional coagulation profile, and normal platelet aggregation studies. Factor assay demonstrated severe factor XIII deficiency (<4%). Inhibitor assay was negative, confirming severe inherited factor XIII deficiency.

She was managed with weekly cryoprecipitate transfusions to maintain factor XIII levels above 15% and tranexamic acid for acute bleeding episodes. She subsequently underwent an elective cesarean section at term without excessive intrapartum or postpartum bleeding.

### Case 2
A 35-year-old second gravida, with no personal or family history of bleeding diathesis and a previous uncomplicated vaginal delivery, was found to have an isolated prolonged activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT; 63 seconds) during routine pre-delivery laboratory evaluation. The prolonged aPTT corrected on mixing studies. Factor XI assay demonstrated severe deficiency (<10%). Next-generation sequencing identified a pathogenic variant in the *F11* gene.

She received prophylactic fresh frozen plasma (FFP) transfusion before induction of labor and subsequently had an uncomplicated vaginal delivery. The postpartum period was uneventful.

## Discussion
These two cases illustrate how rare coagulation factor deficiencies in pregnancy may remain clinically silent until revealed either by obstetric complications or by abnormal antenatal investigations. They also demonstrate two distinct diagnostic pathways. In both women, there was no personal or family history suggestive of a bleeding disorder, and both had previously uncomplicated pregnancies, which could easily have lowered clinical suspicion. However, the current pregnancies revealed subtle but important clues that prompted further evaluation and ultimately led to the diagnosis of rare inherited factor deficiencies.

The first case highlights the importance of considering factor XIII deficiency in women with recurrent antenatal bleeding despite normal conventional coagulation tests. As factor XIII acts after fibrin formation to stabilize the fibrin clot, its deficiency is not detected by routine coagulation screening and requires a specific factor XIII assay for diagnosis.^5^

In this case, persistent bleeding with progressive subchorionic hematoma despite otherwise unremarkable laboratory findings was the key clue. Severe factor XIII deficiency is classically associated with recurrent early pregnancy loss due to defective placental implantation and maintenance. However, our patient had a different phenotype, presenting with persistent antenatal bleeding and an ongoing viable pregnancy rather than recurrent miscarriage. Recognition of this atypical presentation enabled timely replacement therapy with cryoprecipitate and continuation of pregnancy to term. This case underscores an important lesson for obstetricians: normal PT and aPTT values do not exclude an inherited bleeding disorder, and factor XIII deficiency should be considered in women with unexplained recurrent early pregnancy bleeding or pregnancy loss.

In a retrospective French cohort, Rugeri et al.^6^ evaluated 11 women with severe factor XIII deficiency and reported that most pregnancies managed with factor XIII replacement had favorable obstetric outcomes without major hemorrhagic complications, although miscarriage remained common. Similarly, Thangaraj et al.^7^ described factor XIII deficiency in a mother–infant dyad, highlighting its association with recurrent pregnancy loss and neonatal bleeding, including intracranial hemorrhage. Together, these reports reinforce the association between factor XIII deficiency and adverse pregnancy outcomes while emphasizing that timely diagnosis and prophylactic replacement therapy can significantly improve maternal and fetal outcomes. Our case adds to this literature by demonstrating that severe factor XIII deficiency may also present as persistent subchorionic hemorrhage in an ongoing viable pregnancy, even in the absence of prior miscarriage or a prior bleeding history.

The second case represents a contrasting scenario in which the diagnosis arose not from bleeding symptoms but from an isolated prolonged activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT) detected during routine pre-delivery investigations. The prolonged aPTT corrected on mixing studies, indicating a factor deficiency rather than the presence of an inhibitor, and subsequent assay confirmed severe factor XI deficiency. This sequence is clinically important because isolated prolongation of aPTT in pregnancy may be overlooked, especially in women without a bleeding history, or attributed to laboratory artifacts. In this patient, the abnormal aPTT was appropriately followed by a mixing study, which proved to be the pivotal diagnostic step. This case demonstrates that an isolated prolonged aPTT may be the first clue to an intrinsic pathway factor deficiency such as factor XI deficiency. It also emphasizes that previous uncomplicated pregnancies do not exclude an inherited coagulation disorder, particularly in factor XI deficiency, where the bleeding phenotype is variable and correlates poorly with factor levels.

Newman et al.,^8^ in a cohort of 58 women with factor XI deficiency, reported a postpartum hemorrhage rate of 16%. They also found that prophylactic interventions such as tranexamic acid or fresh frozen plasma did not significantly reduce postpartum hemorrhage or transfusion requirements and that factor XI levels at admission correlated poorly with blood loss. Correia et al.^9^ studied 45 pregnancies in women with factor XI deficiency and observed that factor XI levels remained relatively stable throughout pregnancy. Although some women received prophylactic treatment based on low factor levels, prolonged aPTT, or anticipated neuraxial anesthesia, no patient experienced bleeding complications during pregnancy or delivery. These studies illustrate the variable bleeding phenotype in factor XI deficiency and support individualized management based on bleeding history, factor level, and anticipated obstetric interventions rather than laboratory values alone.

These cases highlight the heterogeneity of presentation of rare coagulation factor deficiencies in pregnancy. In the first case, diagnosis required a high index of suspicion despite normal screening tests because the clinical findings were discordant with the laboratory profile. In the second case, diagnosis depended on careful interpretation of an abnormal screening test in an otherwise asymptomatic woman.

Management in both pregnancies was guided by the underlying pathophysiology of the specific deficiency and the anticipated hemostatic challenges of pregnancy and delivery. In the woman with severe factor XIII deficiency, the principal aim was maintenance of pregnancy and prevention of placental bleeding through sustained replacement therapy. In contrast, the woman with factor XI deficiency remained asymptomatic antenatally and required prophylaxis mainly around delivery, when tissue trauma and postpartum uterine involution increase the risk of bleeding.

These cases offer several practical lessons for obstetricians. A previous uncomplicated pregnancy does not exclude an inherited coagulation disorder. Persistent antenatal bleeding with normal routine coagulation studies should prompt further evaluation. Conversely, an isolated prolonged aPTT should always be investigated systematically. Early involvement of maternal-fetal medicine specialists, obstetricians, hematologists, anesthesiologists, and transfusion services is essential for individualized antenatal, intrapartum, and postpartum planning.^10^

In summary, careful interpretation of clinical and laboratory findings, timely use of specific factor assays, and multidisciplinary planning can transform potentially high-risk pregnancies into successful maternal and neonatal outcomes.

## Conclusion
Coagulation factor deficiencies in pregnancy are rare but clinically significant. With careful assessment, regular monitoring, and coordinated multidisciplinary care, most women can achieve safe pregnancies and deliveries. Individualized management is essential because bleeding risk varies widely between disorders and among affected women. Assessment of factor levels during the third trimester, careful planning of delivery, avoidance of traumatic obstetric procedures when possible, and appropriate factor replacement therapy are essential to optimize maternal and neonatal outcomes.
