## Introduction
Tumor lysis syndrome (TLS) is an oncologic emergency resulting from rapid lysis of malignant cells, leading to the release of intracellular metabolites into the circulation.^1^ This causes hyperuricemia, hyperkalemia, hyperphosphatemia, and secondary hypocalcemia, which may precipitate acute kidney injury, cardiac arrhythmias, seizures, and death.^2^

TLS typically occurs following the initiation of chemotherapy in patients with high-grade hematologic malignancies. However, spontaneous tumor lysis syndrome (STLS), which occurs in the absence of cytotoxic therapy, is rare and diagnostically challenging.^3^ It has been described in aggressive malignancies, particularly lymphomas and leukemias, including T-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (T-ALL).^4^

Recognition of STLS prior to a definitive cancer diagnosis is difficult but essential, as delayed management can lead to catastrophic outcomes. We report a case of STLS presenting as the initial manifestation of T-ALL, complicated by severe metabolic derangements and acute kidney injury requiring sustained low-efficiency dialysis.

## Case presentation
A 36-year-old male with no known comorbidities presented with myalgia, vomiting, and low-grade fever. There was no history of weight loss, bleeding manifestations, or prior malignancy.

### Initial evaluation
Laboratory investigations showed:

- Pancytopenia
- Serum creatinine: 4.3 mg/dL
- Serum uric acid: 15.9 mg/dL

Infectious and autoimmune workup was negative. Peripheral smear did not reveal blasts. Bone marrow examination demonstrated trilineage hematopoiesis without evidence of malignancy.

The patient was managed conservatively with intravenous fluids and supportive therapy. Renal parameters improved transiently, and he was discharged.

### Clinical progression
The patient subsequently returned with:

- Generalized lymphadenopathy
- Leukocytosis with atypical lymphocytes
- Progressive renal dysfunction

During evaluation, he developed severe metabolic abnormalities characterized by:

- Serum uric acid: 41 mg/dL
- Serum phosphate: 16 mg/dL
- Serum potassium: 7.9 mmol/L

These findings were consistent with the Cairo–Bishop criteria for spontaneous tumor lysis syndrome.^1^

### Management and diagnosis
The patient was managed with:

- Aggressive intravenous hydration
- Rasburicase
- Febuxostat
- Electrolyte correction
- Isotonic bicarbonate infusion
- Sustained low-efficiency dialysis (SLED)

Following stabilization, further evaluation revealed diffuse positivity for CD3 and strong positivity for terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase (TdT) on flow cytometry.

Lymph node biopsy confirmed T-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia/lymphoma (T-ALL).

He was initiated on a modified Berlin–Frankfurt–Münster (BFM) chemotherapy protocol, which he tolerated well.

## Discussion
TLS results from rapid tumor cell destruction and the release of intracellular contents, particularly nucleic acids which are metabolized into uric acid.^1^ Hyperuricemia and calcium-phosphate precipitation contribute to renal injury.^2^

Spontaneous tumor lysis syndrome (STLS) is uncommon and is thought to result from high tumor proliferation and spontaneous cell turnover exceeding the vascular supply, leading to tumor necrosis.^3^ It is most associated with aggressive hematologic malignancies.^4^

This case highlights several important clinical insights. Severe hyperuricemia in the presence of acute kidney injury should raise suspicion for an underlying occult malignancy. A normal initial bone marrow examination does not exclude evolving leukemia, and STLS may precede the diagnosis of hematologic malignancy. Early administration of rasburicase is crucial in preventing renal failure and other life-threatening complications.^2^

Recognition of STLS requires a high index of suspicion, particularly when metabolic abnormalities are disproportionate to clinical presentation.

## Conclusion
Spontaneous tumor lysis syndrome can be the initial manifestation of aggressive hematologic malignancies such as T-ALL. Early identification and aggressive metabolic management are essential to prevent life-threatening complications. Clinicians should maintain vigilance when encountering unexplained metabolic derangements, even in the absence of confirmed malignancy.
